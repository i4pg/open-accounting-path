### 0.1 — How to Learn Accounting & the Map of the Profession

⏱️ **8-12** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** none

This orientation module gives you the big picture before you touch a single journal entry: how the accounting field is organized into branches (bookkeeping, financial, managerial, tax, audit, accounting information systems, and advisory), who the regulators and standard-setters are, and how the rest of this curriculum maps onto a real credential. You will also build the personal learning machinery — spaced-repetition decks for vocabulary, a problem-first practice loop, an error log, and a public progress log — that makes the later technical modules stick. Finally you learn to find and vet free primary sources so you can tell an authoritative regulator publication from a random blog. It is light on calculation and heavy on orientation, navigation, and habit design.

> **Why it matters:** Knowing the map of the profession turns a long, intimidating syllabus into a route with a destination (a role and a credential), and the study system you build here is what determines whether the next 30 modules actually compound instead of evaporate.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Draw the map of accounting — bookkeeping, financial, managerial, tax, audit, AIS, and advisory — explain how the users and purpose of each branch differ, and locate where automation/AI is reshaping each
- Name the major regulators and standard-setters (FASB/SEC, IASB/IFRS Foundation, IRS, PCAOB/IAASB, AICPA, IMA) and state what each one governs and for whom
- Stand up a personal study system: a spaced-repetition deck for terminology, a problem-first practice routine, an error log, and a public progress log
- Choose a target role and credential (CPA, ACCA, CMA, or professional bookkeeping) and back-map this curriculum's modules onto that exam's sections
- Evaluate the credibility of a free resource by distinguishing primary sources (regulator and standard-setter sites, open textbooks) from secondary commentary

Self-test: given a random accounting term or a news headline about a new rule, you can place it in the right branch and attribute it to the right regulator/standard-setter without looking it up.

### 0.2 — Business & Economics Literacy

⏱️ **14-18** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 0.1

This module builds the business and economic context that accounting exists to measure. You learn what a business actually does — how it creates value, earns revenue, and incurs costs — and how the major legal structures (sole proprietor, partnership, LLC, corporation) shape the way that activity gets recorded and reported. It then traces the operating cycle (buy → make/stock → sell → collect) and connects it to the financial statements, with special attention to the difference between profit and cash. Finally, it grounds you in the core economic ideas (supply and demand, margins, opportunity cost, interest, inflation) that drive every financial decision.

> **Why it matters:** Accounting is the scorekeeping language of business, and numbers are meaningless until you understand the real-world activity and economics behind them. Without this literacy you can record transactions mechanically but never interpret what they say about a company's health or decisions.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Explain what a business does, how it creates value, and how it earns revenue while incurring costs
- Distinguish the major legal forms (sole proprietor, partnership, LLC, corporation) and explain why the choice of structure matters for accounting, taxation, and liability
- Differentiate profit from cash and explain concretely why a profitable business can still run out of money
- Trace the operating cycle from purchasing through sale to cash collection and map each stage onto the financial statements
- Define and apply core economic concepts — supply and demand, margins, opportunity cost, interest, and inflation — that underlie financial decisions

**Resources**

- [BUS101: Introduction to Business](https://learn.saylor.org/course/view.php?id=3)  
  <sub>Saylor Academy · course · 🟢 Free · primary</sub>  
  Free, self-paced anchor for the 'what a business does' half of the module. Work Unit 1 (The Context of Business — business basics, factors of production, how firms create value) and Unit 2 (Entrepreneurship and Legal Forms of Business — sole proprietorship, partnership, LLC, corporation). Skim later marketing/management units. Free final exam and certificate.
- [Principles of Microeconomics 3e](https://openstax.org/details/books/principles-microeconomics-3e)  
  <sub>OpenStax · textbook · 🟢 Free · primary</sub>  
  Free open textbook anchor for the economics half. Read Ch. 1 (scarcity, opportunity cost, choices), Ch. 2 (production possibilities, marginal analysis), and Ch. 3 (demand, supply, market equilibrium). Each chapter has built-in review and self-check questions. Use the OpenStax answer keys to check your work.
- [BUS103: Introduction to Financial Accounting — Operating/Accounting Cycle units](https://learn.saylor.org/course/view.php?id=52)  
  <sub>Saylor Academy · course · 🟢 Free · supplement</sub>  
  Use selectively to connect the operating cycle to the statements. Read the unit overviews and the 'The Accounting Cycle' page; focus on how purchasing, sales, and cash collection flow into the income statement, balance sheet, and statement of cash flows. Do not attempt full double-entry mechanics here — that comes in later modules.
- [Microeconomics: Supply, demand, and market equilibrium](https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/microeconomics/supply-demand-equilibrium)  
  <sub>Khan Academy · video-series · 🟢 Free · supplement</sub>

- [Profit vs. Cash Flow / Why Profitable Companies Run Out of Cash](https://www.youtube.com/@Edspira)  
  <sub>Edspira (YouTube) · video-series · 🟢 Free · supplement</sub>  
  Search the channel for: 'Edspira profit vs cash flow', 'net income vs operating cash flow', and 'Edspira cash conversion cycle / operating cycle'. Crisp explanations by Michael McLaughlin (PhD, CPA) of why net income and cash differ (accruals, receivables, inventory) — the single most important intuition in this module.
- [Khan Academy — Interest and Inflation (Macroeconomics)](https://www.khanacademy.org/economics-finance-domain/macroeconomics)  
  <sub>Khan Academy · video-series · 🟢 Free · supplement</sub>

**🛠️ Project:** Pick one simple real or invented small business (e.g., a coffee cart, a t-shirt reseller, or your own NAWAQIS-style marketplace seller). Produce a 2-3 page 'Business Literacy Brief' that: (1) describes how the business creates value and names its main revenue streams and cost categories; (2) recommends a legal structure (sole prop / partnership / LLC / corporation) with a one-paragraph justification covering liability, taxation, and accounting implications; (3) draws the operating cycle as a simple diagram — purchase inventory → hold → sell → collect cash — and estimates how many days each stage takes; and (4) builds a tiny 3-month numerical example on a spreadsheet showing a month where the business is PROFITABLE on paper but runs NEGATIVE on cash (because a big customer pays late or inventory was bought upfront), then writes 3 sentences explaining why. This forces you to operationalize the profit-vs-cash distinction rather than just read it.

**✅ Checkpoint:** You have mastered this module when you can, without notes: (1) explain in plain language how a business turns inputs into value and where its revenue and costs come from; (2) name the four main legal structures and give one accounting/tax/liability reason each matters; (3) construct a concrete example of a profitable business running out of cash and explain the mechanism (receivables, inventory, or upfront spending); (4) walk a friend through the operating cycle and point to which financial statement each stage touches; and (5) correctly use the terms supply, demand, margin, opportunity cost, interest, and inflation in a sentence about a real pricing or financing decision. Self-test: score 80%+ on the OpenStax Ch. 1-3 review questions and pass the Saylor BUS101 final exam (70%+).

### 0.3 — Spreadsheets for Accountants

⏱️ **22-28** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 0.1

This module builds the practical spreadsheet fluency every accountant needs to turn raw ledger and transaction data into clean, auditable workbooks. You will learn workbook structure and formatting discipline (separating inputs, calculations, and outputs), the core formula toolkit (absolute/relative references, named ranges, SUM/IF/ROUND, lookups, and SUMIFS), and how to summarize data with PivotTables. The module finishes with the two skills that separate accountants from casual spreadsheet users: reconciling two data sets and tracing/auditing formulas to find and trap errors.

> **Why it matters:** Spreadsheets are the universal tool of accounting and finance — reconciliations, schedules, analyses, and ad-hoc reports all live in Excel, and a single broken formula or unverified reference can misstate the numbers a business depends on. Disciplined, auditable workbook skills make your work fast, trustworthy, and reviewable.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Design clean, auditable workbooks: separate inputs/calculations/outputs, use consistent layout and formatting, and apply named ranges and absolute/relative references deliberately
- Apply the core accounting function set fluently: SUM, IF/nested IF/IFS, ROUND, SUMIF(S) and COUNTIF(S), VLOOKUP/XLOOKUP, INDEX/MATCH, and common date and text functions
- Build and refresh PivotTables to summarize general-ledger and transaction extracts by account, period, or dimension
- Reconcile two data sets (e.g., bank vs. book, subledger vs. GL) and use Trace Precedents/Dependents and error checks to find and trap mistakes
- Navigate and edit efficiently with keyboard shortcuts and reduce input errors using data validation

### 0.4 — Business Math & Time Value of Money

⏱️ **18-24** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 0.3

This module builds the quantitative toolkit accountants use every day: percentages, ratios, markups vs. margins, proration, and growth rates, then the core machinery of the time value of money (TVM). ### 1.1 — Principles of Accounting & the Double-Entry System

⏱️ **35-45** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 0.2, 0.3

This module builds the bedrock of all financial accounting: the accounting equation, the five account categories, and the mechanics of double-entry bookkeeping. You learn to analyze transactions, record them with debits and credits in journals and T-accounts, post to the general ledger, and prepare an unadjusted trial balance. It also covers the conceptual framework — the assumptions, principles, and qualitative characteristics shared by GAAP and IFRS — so that the bookkeeping rules make logical sense rather than being memorized.

> **Why it matters:** Every downstream skill in accounting rests on fluently recording and tracing transactions through the double-entry system.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- State the accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity) and explain why every transaction keeps it in balance

- Explain the conceptual framework — economic entity, going concern, monetary unit, cash vs. accrual basis, the matching/revenue-recognition principles, and qualitative characteristics (relevance, faithful representation, comparability) shared by GAAP and IFRS

### 1.3 — Merchandising, Inventory & Cash Controls

⏱️ **28-36** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.2

This module extends the basic accounting cycle to merchandising businesses, where buying and selling inventory drives the income statement. You learn to record purchases, sales, discounts, returns, and freight under both perpetual and periodic systems; apply inventory cost-flow assumptions (FIFO, weighted-average, with LIFO context) and the lower-of-cost-or-net-realizable-value rule; and account for the two largest current assets after inventory — cash and receivables. It closes with the control side of accounting: bank reconciliations, basic internal controls over cash, and estimating uncollectible accounts with the allowance method.

> **Why it matters:** Inventory, cash, and receivables are where most real businesses earn, hold, and lose money, and where the majority of errors and fraud occur — mastering their accounting and controls is the practical heart of day-to-day financial accounting. These mechanics directly determine gross profit, working capital, and the reliability of the numbers every downstream analysis depends on.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Record purchases, sales, discounts, returns, and freight under both perpetual and periodic inventory systems, and compute gross profit
- Apply FIFO and weighted-average cost-flow assumptions to value ending inventory and cost of goods sold, and explain how/why LIFO differs and where it is permitted (US GAAP) vs. prohibited (IFRS)
- Apply the lower-of-cost-or-net-realizable-value rule and record inventory write-downs
- Prepare a bank reconciliation, record the resulting adjusting entries, and identify basic internal controls that safeguard cash
- Account for accounts receivable and estimate bad debts using the allowance method (percentage-of-receivables and percentage-of-sales), contrasting it with the direct write-off method

### 1.4 — Intermediate Financial Accounting I — Assets & Revenue

⏱️ **50-65** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.3

This module takes the asset side of the balance sheet and the top line of the income statement from introductory depth to professional depth. You will master the five-step revenue recognition model (ASC 606 / IFRS 15) for multi-element and over-time contracts, then work through the major asset classes in detail: receivables (estimation and impairment), inventory (costing and lower-of-cost-or-market/NRV), property, plant & equipment (capitalization, depreciation, disposals, impairment), intangibles and goodwill, and short-term/financial investments measured at amortized cost or fair value. Throughout, it highlights the conceptual reasoning behind each measurement choice and flags the key US GAAP versus IFRS differences a preparer or analyst must know.

> **Why it matters:** Revenue and assets are where most reporting judgment — and most financial-statement manipulation — actually lives, so these chapters are the engine room of any preparer, auditor, or analyst role. Mastering recognition, measurement, and impairment here is the difference between reading a balance sheet and truly understanding what it represents.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Apply the five-step revenue recognition model (ASC 606 / IFRS 15) to multi-element arrangements, variable consideration, and over-time (percentage-of-completion) contracts
- Account for receivables in depth, including bad-debt estimation (allowance method), notes receivable, and impairment
- Apply inventory cost-flow assumptions (FIFO, weighted-average) and the lower-of-cost-or-market / net realizable value rule
- Measure and depreciate property, plant & equipment, including cost capitalization, depreciation methods, disposals, and impairment testing
- Account for intangible assets and explain the conceptual treatment and impairment-only model for goodwill
- Measure short-term and financial-asset investments at amortized cost and fair value, identifying the key US GAAP vs. IFRS differences

### 1.5 — Intermediate Financial Accounting II — Liabilities, Equity & Complex Items

⏱️ **50-65** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.4

This module completes the intermediate sequence by tackling the right (financing) side of the balance sheet and the most technically demanding measurement topics. You account for current liabilities, provisions and contingencies, then value and amortize bonds and long-term notes using the effective-interest method. > **Why it matters:** Liabilities, deferred taxes, leases, and equity are where companies exercise the most judgment and where analysts probe hardest, so mastering them is essential for credible financial statement preparation, audit, and valuation work. - Account for income taxes: distinguish temporary vs. permanent differences and measure deferred tax assets/liabilities and valuation allowances
- Account for share capital, treasury stock, cash/stock dividends, stock splits, and stock-based compensation
- Compute and reconcile basic and diluted earnings per share, including the treasury-stock and if-converted methods

### 1.6 — Managerial & Cost Accounting

⏱️ **45-60** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.2

This module shifts from external reporting to the internal accounting that managers use to plan, control, and decide. You will learn how costs behave and are classified (product vs. period; fixed, variable, mixed), and how to assign them through job-order, process, and activity-based costing with overhead allocation. The module then builds the decision and control toolkit: cost-volume-profit and contribution-margin analysis for short-run choices, the full budgeting cycle (master, operating, flexible, and cash budgets), standard-cost variance analysis, and responsibility-accounting tools including transfer pricing, ROI, residual income, and the balanced scorecard.

> **Why it matters:** Managerial accounting is where accounting becomes decision-useful: it powers pricing, make-or-buy, budgeting, and performance evaluation, and is the foundational skill set for FP&A, controller, and operational finance roles.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Classify costs by function and behavior — product vs. period, and fixed/variable/mixed — and separate mixed costs using the high-low method
- Assign costs using job-order, process, and activity-based costing, including predetermined overhead rates and the treatment of over-/under-applied overhead
- Perform cost-volume-profit and contribution-margin analysis to support short-run decisions: make-or-buy, special order, keep-or-drop a segment, and constrained-resource prioritization
- Build a master budget (operating and cash budgets) and use flexible budgets to compute and interpret standard-cost variances for materials, labor, and overhead
- Apply responsibility accounting and decentralized-performance tools — transfer pricing, ROI, residual income, and the balanced scorecard — to evaluate segments and managers

### 1.7 — The Statement of Cash Flows — Deep Dive

⏱️ **12-16** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.2

This module takes the learner from a surface familiarity with the cash flow statement to full preparation fluency under U.S. GAAP. It covers both the indirect and direct methods for the operating section, the T-account/worksheet technique for deriving cash flows from comparative balance sheets and the income statement, correct classification of operating, investing, and financing activities, and the treatment of non-cash investing/financing transactions and supplemental disclosures. By the end, the learner can build a complete Statement of Cash Flows from scratch and explain how it articulates with the other financial statements.

> **Why it matters:** The SCF is one of the most heavily tested topics on the CPA FAR and BAR exams, where candidates are routinely asked to classify items, convert between methods, and reconstruct cash flows from account changes. Mastering the worksheet reconciliation is also a core skill in financial analysis, audit, and FP&A roles, where understanding cash generation separates accounting literacy from real competence.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Prepare a complete Statement of Cash Flows using the INDIRECT method, starting from net income and adjusting for non-cash items, gains/losses, and changes in operating working-capital accounts
- Prepare the operating section using the DIRECT method (cash collected from customers, cash paid to suppliers/employees, interest, and taxes) and reconcile it to the indirect-method total
- Use a T-account/worksheet to derive each cash flow line item from comparative balance sheets and the income statement
- Correctly classify transactions as operating, investing, or financing activities under U.S. GAAP, including interest, dividends, and taxes
- Identify and report non-cash investing and financing activities and the required supplemental disclosures (interest paid, income taxes paid)
- Explain how the SCF articulates with the income statement and the comparative balance sheets, and prove that the net change in cash ties to the balance sheet

### 1.8 — Investments & Equity-Method Accounting

⏱️ **14-18** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.5

How a company accounts for the securities and ownership stakes it holds in OTHER entities. You learn the three classifications of investments in debt and equity securities (trading/FVTPL, available-for-sale/FVOCI, and held-to-maturity/amortized cost under ASC 320/321 and IFRS 9), and — most importantly — the EQUITY METHOD used when an investor has significant influence (typically 20–50% ownership; ASC 323 / IAS 28). You record purchases, fair-value remeasurements, the investor's share of investee income, and dividends, and you maintain the investment-carrying-value rollforward.

> **Why it matters:** The equity method and the fair-value/amortized-cost classifications are directly tested on CPA FAR and CMA Part 1, and — critically — equity-method and business-combination mechanics are the hard PREREQUISITE for Consolidations (2.1). Skip this and consolidations will feel like magic instead of arithmetic.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Classify an investment in securities as trading/FVTPL, available-for-sale/FVOCI, or held-to-maturity/amortized cost and record the correct fair-value or amortized-cost measurement and where gains/losses are reported (net income vs OCI)
- Apply the EQUITY METHOD: record the initial investment, the investor's proportionate share of investee net income (increasing the carrying value), and dividends received (decreasing it), and prepare the investment-account rollforward
- Explain when significant influence (vs. control vs. passive holding) determines the accounting method, and how the threshold maps to fair value, equity method, and consolidation
- Contrast the US GAAP (ASC 320/321/323) and IFRS (IFRS 9 / IAS 28) treatments at a working level
- Articulate the bridge from a >50% controlling investment to consolidation, setting up Module 2.1

### 1.10 — Accounting for Income Taxes (Deferred Taxes)

⏱️ **14-18** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.5

FINANCIAL-STATEMENT tax accounting under ASC 740 / IAS 12 — distinct from preparing a tax return (that's Module 4.1). You learn why book income and taxable income differ, the split between TEMPORARY differences (which reverse and create deferred tax assets and liabilities) and PERMANENT differences (which never reverse and only affect the effective rate), how to recognize and measure DTAs/DTLs, when a valuation allowance is needed against a DTA, how enacted tax-rate changes remeasure deferred balances, and how to compute the total tax provision (current + deferred) and the effective-tax-rate reconciliation.

> **Why it matters:** Accounting for income taxes is the single most commonly omitted topic in self-study tracks and one of the most heavily tested on CPA FAR (and CMA Part 1). It also unlocks reading any real 10-K's tax footnote. Pairs with tax compliance (4.1) so you see book-vs-tax from both sides.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Distinguish temporary differences (depreciation, warranty/unearned-revenue accruals, etc.) from permanent differences (municipal-bond interest, fines, certain meals) and explain why only temporary differences create deferred taxes
- Recognize and measure deferred tax assets and liabilities from temporary differences using the enacted tax rate
- Assess whether a valuation allowance is required against a deferred tax asset (the 'more likely than not' threshold)
- Remeasure deferred tax balances for an enacted change in tax rates and record the effect
- Compute the total income tax provision (current tax payable + change in deferred) and prepare the effective-tax-rate reconciliation that explains the gap between the statutory and effective rates

### 1.11 — Payroll Accounting

⏱️ **12-16 hours** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.2

This module covers the full payroll cycle from a bookkeeper's seat: computing gross pay, subtracting employee withholdings (federal/state income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and voluntary deductions) to reach net pay, and separately accounting for employer-borne payroll taxes (matching FICA, FUTA, SUTA). Learners record the two distinct journal entries payroll generates — the wages/withholdings entry and the employer payroll-tax entry — then track the resulting payroll liabilities, accrue wages across period-ends, and reconcile those liability accounts back to the general ledger and the payroll register. The module also introduces the basic mechanics a bookkeeper uses to record indirect taxes (sales tax / VAT / GST) as a collected liability rather than revenue.

> **Why it matters:** Payroll is one of the most common and highest-stakes tasks a working bookkeeper performs, and gross-to-net pay, payroll journal entries, and payroll-liability reconciliation are explicitly tested on both the AIPB Certified Bookkeeper and NACPB Certified Public Bookkeeper exams. Mishandled withholdings or unremitted payroll-tax liabilities create real legal and cash exposure for employers, so demonstrable competence here is a direct employability signal.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Compute gross pay for hourly (including overtime) and salaried employees and reduce it to net pay by applying federal/state income tax, Social Security (6.2%), Medicare (1.45%), and voluntary deductions
- Distinguish employee withholdings (the employer holds and remits) from employer payroll taxes (matching FICA, FUTA, SUTA) that are an additional expense to the employer
- Prepare the two distinct payroll journal entries — one for wages expense and withholding liabilities, one for employer payroll-tax expense — and the later entries that remit those liabilities and pay employees
- Accrue wages and payroll taxes at period-end and recognize the related current liabilities on the balance sheet
- Reconcile payroll-liability general-ledger accounts to the payroll register and to amounts actually remitted
- Record indirect/sales tax (sales tax, VAT, or GST) collected on sales as a Sales Tax Payable liability and account for its remittance, recognizing it is not revenue

### 2.1 — Advanced Financial Accounting — Consolidations & FX

⏱️ **48-58** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.5

This module covers the hardest core of financial reporting: combining a parent and its subsidiaries into a single set of statements. You apply the acquisition method (computing goodwill, non-controlling interest, and fair-value step-ups), eliminate intercompany transactions, balances, and unrealized profit, and translate the statements of a foreign subsidiary between functional and reporting currencies (with an introduction to hedging). > **Why it matters:** Almost every public company and most mid-market groups report on a consolidated basis with foreign operations, so consolidation and FX translation are the gatekeeping skills for financial-reporting roles, audit, M&A analysis, and CPA/ACCA exam readiness.

- Apply the acquisition method end to end: measure goodwill (or a bargain purchase), value non-controlling interest, push down fair-value adjustments, and prepare a consolidated balance sheet and income statement

- Record foreign-currency transactions and translate a foreign subsidiary using the current-rate (translation) and temporal (remeasurement) methods, computing the cumulative translation adjustment, with an introduction to FX hedging
- Account for equity-method investments and identify when a variable-interest entity must be consolidated
- Account for partnerships across their life cycle: formation, profit-and-loss allocation, admission and withdrawal of partners, and liquidation

### 2.4 — Auditing & Assurance

⏱️ **42-52** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 2.3, 2.2

This module covers external financial-statement auditing and the broader assurance continuum: the audit objective, materiality, reasonable assurance, independence, and professional skepticism. You learn the audit risk model (inherent, control, detection risk), how to evaluate internal control using the COSO framework, and how to design tests of controls and substantive procedures — including audit sampling and full-population data testing. The module culminates in gathering and documenting evidence, forming an audit opinion, and selecting the correct report type. Throughout, you treat standards as jurisdiction-specific (ISA vs. PCAOB/US GAAS) — learning the universal concepts first, then mapping them to your local standard-setter.

> **Why it matters:** Assurance is the trust layer that makes financial statements usable by investors, lenders, and regulators; understanding how auditors think also makes you a stronger preparer who can anticipate scrutiny and build defensible records. It is a gateway competency for the CPA/ACCA/CA credential and for any controllership, internal-audit, or risk role.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Explain the assurance continuum, the audit objective, materiality, reasonable (vs. absolute) assurance, independence, and professional skepticism, and tie management assertions to audit objectives
- Apply the audit risk model (AR = IR x CR x DR) and evaluate internal control over financial reporting using the five COSO components
- Design and perform tests of controls and substantive procedures, selecting appropriate evidence types and applying both statistical/non-statistical sampling and full-population data analytics
- Document work in audit working papers and evaluate sufficiency and appropriateness of evidence to support conclusions
- Form the appropriate audit opinion (unmodified, qualified, adverse, disclaimer) and identify the report modifications and emphasis/key-audit-matter paragraphs each situation requires
- Distinguish ISA from PCAOB/US GAAS requirements and locate the authoritative standard governing a given procedure in your jurisdiction

### 4.1 — Income Taxation Fundamentals (Jurisdiction-Specific)

⏱️ **40-52** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.5

- Compute and contrast entity taxation across C corporations (entity-level tax plus shareholder-level tax on distributions), S corporations, and partnerships (pass-through), and identify each entity's filing obligations
- Explain consumption taxes (VAT/GST/sales tax), the input/output credit mechanics, and how output tax payable and recoverable input tax are recorded and settled in the books
- Adapt the framework to your own country's tax code, mapping local rules onto the universal concepts of base, rate, timing, and entity classification

### 4.2 — Business Law for Accountants (Jurisdiction-Specific)

⏱️ **26-36** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 0.2

The goal is legal literacy for accountants — enough to spot issues, read a contract or security agreement intelligently, and know when to involve counsel — not to make you a lawyer.

> **Why it matters:** Accountants constantly act on legal facts — whether a contract is enforceable, who is liable in an LLC vs. a partnership, whether a creditor is perfected, when auditor liability attaches — and getting them wrong creates restatements, fraud exposure, and malpractice claims. It is also a heavily tested domain on the CPA REG exam, so mastering it pays off twice.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Explain contract formation, performance, breach, and remedies, and apply agency and UCC Article 2 sale-of-goods rules at a working level

- Describe debtor-creditor relationships, the attachment and perfection of security interests under UCC Article 9, the basics of bankruptcy, and negotiable instruments
- Summarize federal securities regulation (1933/1934 Acts), anti-fraud provisions, and the bases of accountants' professional and legal liability
- Identify the regulatory bodies and statutes governing accountants and financial reporting in your own jurisdiction, mapping them to the common-law/US framework studied here

### 4.3 — Corporate Finance Essentials

⏱️ **30-40** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 2.2

This module takes you from accounting numbers to value-based decision-making. You learn the time value of money and apply it to capital budgeting (NPV, IRR, payback, profitability index) with sensitivity/scenario analysis, then estimate a firm's cost of capital (WACC) and reason about capital structure and dividend policy. It rounds out with working-capital management (cash, receivables, inventory, short-term financing) and a first pass at business valuation using discounted cash flow and comparable companies, always tying financing and investing choices back to the financial statements and underlying value drivers.

> **Why it matters:** Accountants who understand corporate finance can move beyond recording transactions to advising on which projects to fund, how to finance them, and what a business is worth — the skills that turn a bookkeeper into a finance business partner.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Apply time value of money to capital budgeting: compute and rank projects by NPV, IRR, payback, and profitability index, and stress-test results with sensitivity and scenario analysis

- Manage working capital — cash, accounts receivable, inventory, and short-term financing — using metrics like the cash conversion cycle
- Build a basic discounted-cash-flow valuation and cross-check it with a comparable-company (multiples) analysis
- Connect financing and investing decisions back to the financial statements and the firm's underlying value drivers

### 4.4 — Accounting Ethics & Professional Responsibility

⏱️ **15-20** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 2.4

This module builds the ethical reasoning and professional-responsibility competencies that distinguish accounting as a regulated profession from a mere technical skill. You will learn to apply a structured ethical-decision framework together with the major professional codes of conduct (AICPA in the U.S. and the IESBA/IFAC International Code globally), reason through the core threats to integrity — independence, objectivity, confidentiality, conflicts of interest, and the overriding public-interest duty — and recognize the pressures that drive earnings management and fraud (the fraud triangle). It also covers the regulatory architecture that holds accountants accountable (SOX, PCAOB, whistleblower protections, due care) and the landmark scandals (Enron, WorldCom, and more recent cases) that produced those reforms, along with the professional-liability exposure practitioners must manage.

> **Why it matters:** A single ethical lapse can void a career, collapse a firm, and destroy investor trust — Arthur Andersen vanished over Enron — so employers, regulators, and the CPA/CMA exams all treat ethics as a non-negotiable core competency rather than an afterthought. Mastering a defensible decision framework lets you spot and resist real-world pressure to misstate results before it becomes a violation.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Apply a structured ethical-decision framework alongside a professional code (AICPA Code of Professional Conduct and the IESBA/IFAC International Code) to resolve realistic accounting dilemmas and justify the chosen action
- Explain and distinguish the fundamental principles — integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, and professional behavior — and the special role of independence and the public-interest duty in assurance work
- Use the conceptual 'threats and safeguards' approach to identify self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, and intimidation threats and apply appropriate safeguards
- Recognize earnings-management and fraud incentives through the fraud triangle (pressure, opportunity, rationalization) and explain the regulatory response — SOX, the PCAOB, whistleblower protections, and due care
- Analyze landmark scandals (Enron, WorldCom, and a modern case) to connect specific failures to the reforms they produced
- Identify sources of professional-liability exposure (negligence, breach of contract, fraud) and describe practical ways practitioners manage and limit that risk

### 5.1 — Governmental & Nonprofit Accounting (Elective)

⏱️ **25-35** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 1.5

This elective extends financial accounting into the public and not-for-profit sectors, where the goal is accountability for restricted resources rather than profit. You learn fund accounting and the modified-accrual basis used in governmental funds, how budgets and encumbrances are recorded, and how to build both fund and government-wide financial statements under the GASB reporting model (with a brief contrast to the international IPSAS framework). The module then turns to nonprofit organizations — net asset classification, accounting for contributions, and the functional (program vs. supporting) expense reporting required of charities.

> **Why it matters:** Governments and nonprofits control a large share of economic activity, and their accounting rules (GASB, fund accounting, Form 990) are fundamentally different from for-profit GAAP — fluency here opens auditing, public-sector, and nonprofit-finance careers and is a tested area on the CPA FAR exam.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Explain fund accounting, fund categories, and the modified-accrual (current-financial-resources) measurement focus, and contrast them with for-profit accrual accounting

- Prepare governmental fund statements and convert/reconcile them to government-wide full-accrual statements under the GASB model, and describe how IPSAS differs
- Account for nonprofit net asset classes (with and without donor restrictions), conditional vs. unconditional contributions, and present expenses by function in a statement of functional expenses

### 5.2 — Forensic Accounting & Fraud Examination (Elective)

⏱️ **25-35** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 2.4, 3.3

This elective teaches how occupational and financial-statement fraud is perpetrated, concealed, detected, and proven. You will learn the standard fraud taxonomy (asset misappropriation, corruption, financial-statement fraud) and the behavioral models behind it (the fraud triangle/diamond), then move into hands-on detection using data analytics (Benford's Law, ratio and anomaly analysis) and investigative skills (evidence handling, interviewing). The module closes the loop from suspicion to courtroom: gathering litigation-grade evidence, quantifying damages, writing a defensible expert report, and designing anti-fraud controls and a fraud risk assessment.

> **Why it matters:** Fraud costs organizations an estimated 5% of revenue every year, and the people who can both detect it analytically and document it to an evidentiary standard are rare and highly paid. This skill set turns an accountant from a recorder of transactions into an investigator who protects the enterprise and can support litigation.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Classify any fraud scheme onto the ACFE Fraud Tree (asset misappropriation, corruption, financial-statement fraud) and explain it through the fraud triangle (pressure, opportunity, rationalization)
- Apply data-analytic detection techniques — Benford's Law, ratio/horizontal/vertical analysis, duplicate and gap testing, and anomaly detection — to a transaction dataset
- Plan and conduct a fraud investigation: develop a fraud theory, gather and preserve evidence to a litigation-ready standard, and structure an admission-seeking interview
- Quantify fraud losses/damages and write a clear, defensible forensic and expert-witness report
- Design layered anti-fraud controls and perform a basic fraud risk assessment for a business process

### 5.3 — International Accounting & IFRS Deep Dive (Elective)

⏱️ **25-35** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 2.1

- Account for cross-border issues including functional vs. presentation currency translation and segment reporting
- Explain the global standard-setting landscape — the IFRS Foundation, IASB, due process, and the history and current state of IFRS–US GAAP convergence

## Track 6 — Capstone & Certification

### 6.1 — Capstone — Full-Cycle Engagement, Analysis & Certification

⏱️ **50-70** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 3.1, 2.2, 2.4, 4.4

This capstone integrates everything from the curriculum into one end-to-end engagement: you take a fictional company from raw transactions through the full accounting cycle and period-end close to a complete, footnoted, standards-compliant set of financial statements built inside real cloud accounting software. You then layer on the professional-judgment deliverables an employer expects — an analysis-and-recommendation memo, an automated reporting dashboard, an internal-controls and audit-risk write-up, and an ethics and tax/compliance summary — and assemble it all into a public portfolio. The module closes with a personal credentialing plan that maps your chosen certification (CPA, ACCA, CMA, or a bookkeeping credential) to a concrete study schedule.

> **Why it matters:** A polished, public capstone is the single strongest signal of job-ready competence for a self-taught accountant, because it demonstrates the full cycle plus judgment rather than isolated skills. Pairing it with a realistic credential plan turns "I studied accounting" into a concrete path to a hireable, licensable career.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Process a full period of transactions for a fictional company in cloud accounting software, complete the period-end close, and produce a footnoted, standards-compliant set of financial statements (income statement, balance sheet, cash-flow statement, and equity statement with disclosures)
- Produce a financial-analysis-and-recommendation memo using ratio, trend, and common-size analysis, supported by an automated reporting/analytics dashboard
- Document the engagement's internal controls, an audit-risk assessment, an ethics reflection against a professional code of conduct, and a tax/compliance summary
- Assemble a public portfolio (workpapers, statements, models, dashboards, and exported software files) that demonstrates full-cycle, job-ready competence
- Choose a target credential (CPA, ACCA, CMA, or a bookkeeping credential), confirm eligibility against official requirements, and build a gap-to-schedule study plan with practice exams

### 6.2 — Exam Readiness & Practice Simulations

⏱️ **40-55** · 🔗 **Prerequisites:** 6.1

This module converts accumulated subject knowledge into pass-readiness for the certifications this curriculum advertises (CPA, CMA, EA, ACCA, and bookkeeping certificates). Learners locate the official free sample tests, blueprints, and retired-question banks published by each exam body, then learn to build a topic-to-blueprint study plan and sit full mock exams under realistic timed conditions. It covers the distinct question formats — timed MCQ, CPA task-based simulations (TBS), CMA essays, and EA representation scenarios — and emphasizes diagnosing wrong answers by root cause. EA candidates additionally practice Circular 230 representation and practitioner-conduct questions.

> **Why it matters:** Knowing the material and passing a timed, format-specific professional exam are different skills, and the credential — not the coursework — is what employers and state boards recognize. Deliberate timed simulation against the official blueprint is the single highest-leverage predictor of a first-attempt pass.

**By the end you'll be able to:**

- Locate and download the official, free blueprint/content outline and sample test for at least one target credential (CPA, CMA, EA, ACCA, or a bookkeeping certificate) and map every tested area back to the curriculum modules that cover it
- Build a dated, hours-budgeted study plan that sequences weakest-area-first and schedules at least two full mock sittings before the exam date
- Sit a full sample/mock exam under strict timed, single-session, closed-note conditions and pace MCQs to the per-question time budget
- Work the non-MCQ formats: complete a CPA task-based simulation, draft a CMA essay response, and answer an EA representation scenario
- Apply Circular 230 rules to representation questions — who may practice before the IRS, the scope of EA representation, and practitioner duties/sanctions
- Diagnose every missed question by root cause (knowledge gap vs. misread vs. timing) and convert the diagnosis into a targeted remediation list
- Score a mock against the official passing standard and write a defensible go/no-go readiness decision
